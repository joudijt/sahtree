Adding Arabic Gum Drinks to a Café Menu: A Malaysian F&B Guide

August 25, 2026 · 6 min read · By Sihatree

A drinks menu is one of the more natural places for a café to test a new ingredient.

Arabic Gum Powder dissolves fully into cold or room-temperature drinks, leaving no gritty texture and no strong flavour of its own, so a café can add it to an existing drinks menu without reformulating a recipe. That's the practical detail that matters most to an F&B owner weighing whether it's worth testing — not a health claim, just a beverage-program property.

Why would a café consider adding an Arabic Gum drink to its menu?

Malaysian consumers have shown growing interest in Arabic Gum over the past while — see our piece on why it's becoming more visible in Malaysia for that context — and a café is one of the places customers are likely to look for it once they've heard of it. A drinks menu is also a low-friction way to test a new ingredient: unlike adding a whole new dish, it doesn't require new equipment, a new prep station, or retraining kitchen staff. If it doesn't perform, dropping it from the menu costs nothing beyond the stock on hand.

Does Arabic Gum change the taste of a drink?

Not noticeably, in most cases. Arabic Gum (gum acacia) dissolves fully in cold or room-temperature liquid on stirring — it's milled to a fine, instant powder, so there's no gritty residue settling at the bottom of a glass and no soaking step. It also carries no strong flavour of its own, which is why it can generally be stirred into an existing recipe — a fruit drink, an infused water, a fruit tea — rather than requiring a drink to be built around it. A café should still taste-test it in its own recipes before committing it to a printed menu; "no strong flavour" is a general property, not a guarantee for every recipe and every palate.

How much goes into one drink, and does that help with consistent prep at volume?

Sihatree's own packaging guidance is to stir one scoop into 250–300ml of water or a drink of choice, once or twice daily, and it dissolves fully. That's a consumer serving guideline, not a food-service formulation — but the underlying property is genuinely useful for a beverage program: a fixed scoop-to-liquid ratio gives a barista a repeatable starting point to standardise across shifts, rather than eyeballing an ingredient that behaves differently batch to batch. A café would still want to test that ratio against its own cup sizes and existing recipes before locking in a house measurement — what dissolves cleanly in a 250–300ml glass at home won't automatically translate 1:1 into a 500ml iced cup or a pitcher-batched infused water.

Is Arabic Gum a relevant consideration for a Halal-conscious customer base?

For a Malaysian F&B business, yes — it's worth understanding accurately rather than assuming. Arabic Gum is plant-derived, with no alcohol and no animal-derived ingredients, which is a genuinely relevant fact for a Halal-conscious menu. At the same time, Sihatree does not hold a JAKIM Halal certificate, and that's worth stating plainly to any café evaluating it: a business that specifically requires certified stock for its own menu labelling should treat the absence of a certificate as the deciding fact, not something to work around. A café is free to describe the ingredient accurately to its own customers, but shouldn't claim a certification that doesn't exist.
