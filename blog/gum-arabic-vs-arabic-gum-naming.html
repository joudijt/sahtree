Gum Arabic vs Arabic Gum: Same Thing, Different Name?

September 10, 2026 · 7 min read · By Sihatree

One powder, several names depending on who printed the label.

Gum arabic vs Arabic gum is a naming difference, not an ingredient difference: both names describe the dried, purified sap of the Acacia tree. The word order changes with the market. Gum arabic is the trade name used across the food industry; Arabic Gum is the phrasing Malaysian shoppers say, and the one on the Sihatree pouch.

Does the word order change what is inside the jar?

No. Gum arabic and Arabic gum name one material: sap that seeps from the bark of Acacia senegal or Acacia seyal, hardens, is cleaned and milled into a water-soluble powder. Two packs printed with the two word orders can hold contents identical down to the tree they were tapped from.

What the material is, and how much people use, sits in the full guide to what Arabic Gum is.

Which name appears on which kind of label?

It depends on who wrote the label. Food manufacturers and technical documents use gum arabic. American and European panels often print acacia gum or gum acacia. A short additive list may show only E414. Retail fibre tubs say acacia fiber. Malaysian packs, Sihatree included, say Arabic Gum.

Tip

The front of a pack is marketing; the back is regulated labelling. The ingredient line settles a disagreement.

What does E414 on an ingredient panel tell you?

That this gum is in the product, whatever the front of the pack calls it. E414 is the food-additive number for the material, and a number reads the same in every language, so it matches a Malaysian pouch against a European pack printed in French.

The gum is roughly 85% soluble fibre. It holds GRAS status in the United States, and EFSA and JECFA have assessed it as a food additive: safety assessments, not findings about health outcomes, as what the evidence on acacia gum supports sets out.

Is acacia fiber the same product as gum arabic?

On a shop shelf, usually yes. Acacia fiber is a retail name for the milled gum sold as a dietary fibre, and the ingredient behind that label is the same acacia sap. Read the ingredient line, not the product name: it should say gum arabic, acacia gum or E414.

Why does the Malaysian pack say Arabic Gum instead of gum arabic?

Because that is the word order shoppers here use. The Malay name is gam arab, and Arabic Gum is its closest English reading, so Sihatree Arabic Gum Powder carries it on a 150 g pouch in five flavours. Inside is what a European panel would list as gum arabic or E414.

How do you check two packs hold the same ingredient?

Compare ingredient lines, not front-of-pack names. If both read gum arabic, acacia gum, acacia fiber or E414, you hold one material under two naming conventions. If one lists guar gum, xanthan gum or psyllium husk instead, that is a different plant and a different fibre.

Stop treating two word orders as two products. Check the ingredient line, then compare on form and fibre: start with acacia gum against guar gum.
