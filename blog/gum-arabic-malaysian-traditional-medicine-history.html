Gum Arabic in Malaysian Traditional Medicine: History & Uses

July 16, 2026 · 6 min read · By Sihatree

Long before it was a daily wellness drink, gum arabic had a place in traditional herbal practice.

Gum arabic has a long history of traditional use across Middle Eastern, North African, and Asian herbal medicine, historically valued as a soothing, binding agent. Today it's recognised primarily as a natural dietary fibre rather than a medicine — here's the history, and what actually holds up under modern science.

A brief history of gum arabic in traditional medicine

Gum arabic's story as a traditional remedy goes back centuries before it became a packaged wellness product.

Ancient Middle Eastern and North African origins

Harvested from Acacia trees across the Middle East and North Africa, gum arabic has documented use going back to ancient Egyptian and Arab herbal traditions, where it was valued for its soothing texture and binding properties in traditional remedies.

Its place in Malay traditional herbal practice

Gum arabic itself arrived in the Malay Archipelago as a traded ingredient rather than a native one, but it found a natural place within perubatan tradisional Melayu (traditional Malay medicine), which has its own long history of using plant gums, resins, and mucilage-rich ingredients for similar soothing and binding roles in traditional preparations.

Traditional uses across cultures

Soothing the throat

Traditionally dissolved in water as a soothing drink for throat discomfort.

Digestive comfort

Used traditionally to settle the stomach, long before its fibre content was scientifically understood.

Traditional wound care

Historically used as a binding agent in some traditional topical preparations.

Traditional cosmetic use

Featured in some historical skin preparations for its binding, smoothing texture.

Binding agent in remedies

Used to hold other herbal ingredients together in traditional pastes and mixtures.

Modern dietary fibre

Today, its best-supported role is as a natural soluble prebiotic fibre in the daily diet.

What modern science actually supports

Historical use isn't the same as proven benefit

Many traditional uses listed above are historical and cultural in nature, not clinically proven medical treatments. What's actually well-supported by modern research is gum arabic's role as a soluble prebiotic fibre — this is a separate, better-established benefit from its older traditional-medicine reputation, and it's the reason Sihatree sells it today.
